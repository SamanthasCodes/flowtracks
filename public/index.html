About

Every minute, one garbage truck of plastic enters our oceans. This pollution can travel far and wide, from high mountain environments to the deep sea, impacting even the most remote locations. Tracking plastic pollution helps reveal how debris moves through the ocean, where it concentrates, and where cleanup or prevention efforts may have the greatest impact.

Once in the oceans, plastic swiftly flows into a restless system of currents, eddies, and turbulence. Broad circulation patterns transport debris across long distances and influence where plastic accumulates, while smaller turbulent motions stir particles through the surrounding water and shape how widely they disperse. Together, these dynamics make it difficult to track plastic's path, model where it will collect, and implement targeted cleanup interventions.

FlowTracks is an interactive simulation that lets users explore these dynamics directly. By releasing virtual particles and adjusting turbulence, users can observe how ocean mixing shapes where pollution travels, how widely it spreads, and where it may accumulate. Rather than treating plastic pollution as distant or abstract, FlowTracks invites users to experiment with the movement patterns that make ocean plastic so difficult to understand and address.

Within the simulation, country colors show projected annual marine plastic debris entering the ocean in 2025, measured in tonnes per year. The ocean current overlay traces the surface system that carries debris once it enters the water. For a full walkthrough of the simulation controls and features, visit How to Use.

Each dot drifting across the map gives form to something that is otherwise invisible: a piece of plastic pollution — a forgotten bottle, a lost fishing net, a straw carried out to sea by the same currents you see on the map. How to Use

Click anywhere on the ocean map to release one cloud of virtual plastic particles. Each particle acts as a tracer for floating plastic carried by ocean motion.

Only one particle cloud can be active at a time. Wait for the current cloud to finish dispersing before releasing another, or press Reset to clear the simulation.

Use the control panel to adjust turbulence, particle lifetime, particle count, trajectories, current arrows, and display resolution. Clicking on land will not release particles.

Data & Methods

FlowTracks simulates buoyant plastic fragments carried by ocean surface currents and visualizes projected marine plastic debris entering the ocean in 2025. The simulation combines country-level estimates of annual marine plastic debris with ocean current data to help users explore how plastic pollution may move, spread, and accumulate once it enters the marine environment.

Ocean plastic transport operates across two distinct spatial scales. Large-scale circulation patterns, such as gyres and boundary currents, operate at the scale of hundreds of kilometers, transporting debris across ocean basins over weeks to months. Smaller turbulent motions, including eddies and mixing processes, operate at the scale of hundreds of meters, redistributing particles through the surrounding water and driving lateral dispersion within a cloud of debris.

In FlowTracks, these dynamics are represented through two parameters users can control directly. The ocean current field drives large-scale advection, moving particles along realistic surface current pathways derived from OSCAR satellite data. The diffusion slider represents small-scale turbulence, adding a random walk component to each particle's trajectory at every time step. Higher diffusion values produce wider, faster-spreading clouds; lower values keep particles more tightly clustered along the dominant current path. Together, these two components allow users to observe how the interplay between large-scale transport and small-scale mixing shapes where plastic accumulates and how widely it disperses.

Design Approach

FlowTracks was designed to guide users through a progressive experience, moving from emotional hook to scientific exploration. The loading page opens with a single striking statistic, a one-line context sentence, and a direct personal question, inviting users into the problem before any interaction begins. The About page then introduces the environmental stakes using accessible language grounded in scientific concepts, before the simulation itself hands control to the user. This sequencing was intentional: understanding why ocean plastic is difficult to track makes the act of releasing and watching particles feel meaningful rather than abstract.

The visual language was built around two accent colors that carry consistent meaning throughout the interface. Toxic green represents pollution, used for particle trails, choropleth intensity, highlighted statistics, and the Reset button. Teal represents information and interaction, used for navigation, current labels, control highlights, and explanatory text. Together these sit against a deep ocean gradient that moves from lighter blue at the top to near-black at the bottom, creating a sense of depth and unease. Typography uses Cormorant SC and Cormorant serif throughout, chosen for their editorial quality and subtle tension between beauty and gravity.

Interactivity was designed to build intuition rather than simply display data. Hovering over ocean current labels reveals their names; hovering over countries surfaces their projected plastic contribution; hovering over controls explains what each parameter does. The particle spread plot lets users compare multiple releases and observe directly how turbulence and release location shape dispersal patterns. Every design choice, from the constrained particle animation in the margins to the choropleth's dark-to-bright green scale, was made to help users feel, not just read, how their own actions and the ocean's dynamics together determine where plastic goes.
